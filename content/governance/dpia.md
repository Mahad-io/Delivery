## 1. Is a DPIA required?

Strictly, no. This processing does not meet the Art. 35(3) triggers or the ICO's list of high-risk processing: there is no systematic monitoring of a publicly accessible area, no large-scale special category data, no automated decision-making with legal effect, no profiling, no children's data as a target audience, no biometrics, no data matching, and no innovative technology.

We completed one anyway. It is a short document for a small service, and the exercise found three design changes worth making, which is a better return than most compliance activity.

## 2. Describe the processing

**Nature.** A public website publishes delivery-management templates and learning material. Content is fully readable anonymously. An optional free account gates downloads and persistence. Collected data: identity (name, email), authentication (password hash), optional profile, and activity generated by using the service (downloads, saved items, learning progress, assessment answers, retro notes).

**Scope.** Expected to be low thousands of registered users, all adults, all using the service in a professional context. No special category data is collected. Data is held in a single SQLite database on one server in the UK.

**Context.** Users are professionals choosing to use a free resource site. The relationship is low-stakes: there is no service they are compelled to use, no benefit contingent on it, and no consequence to walking away. That materially lowers the risk profile compared with, say, a public service or an employer's system.

**Purposes.** Provide the account features; understand aggregate demand for content; keep the service secure.

## 3. Consultation

- **Data subjects:** the privacy notice, ROPA and this DPIA are published, and the feedback route is on every page. Any substantive comment received will be recorded here at the annual review.
- **Internal:** reviewed by the person accountable for the service and the person accountable for data protection.
- **Processors:** hosting and email providers were selected partly on their published security posture and Art. 28 terms.
- **ICO:** not consulted. Not required, as no residual high risk remains after mitigation.

## 4. Necessity and proportionality

| Question | Assessment |
| --- | --- |
| Is the lawful basis appropriate? | Yes. Contract for account operation, consent for optional fields and marketing, legitimate interests for security and aggregate counts, legal obligation for consent records. Documented per activity in the ROPA |
| Is the processing necessary for the purpose? | Yes for account data. The three optional profile fields are *not* necessary, which is precisely why they are optional and consent-based |
| Is it proportionate? | Yes. Content is readable without an account, so no one is compelled to give data to get the substantive benefit. The gate is only on download and persistence |
| Data minimisation | Mandatory fields reduced to email, name, password. No IP addresses stored. No free text in the assessment. No browsing history stored per page |
| Accuracy | Users can correct their own profile at any time |
| Purpose limitation | Data is not used for anything beyond the stated purposes. No model training, no profiling, no sale, no sharing |
| Transparency | Privacy notice written to be read; ROPA and this DPIA published; consent history visible in the export |
| Rights facilitation | Access and erasure are self-service and immediate. No form, no identity check beyond being signed in, no waiting period |
| Processor compliance | Art. 28 terms in place; sub-processors published |

## 5. Risks identified, and what we did

| # | Risk | Likelihood | Severity | Mitigation | Residual |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| R1 | Credential stuffing exposes accounts because users reuse passwords | Medium | Medium | 12-character minimum; common-password screening; per-account lockout after 8 failures; per-IP rate limiting; activity log visible to the user; no password hints or security questions | Low |
| R2 | Database compromise exposes email addresses and hashes | Low | High | bcrypt cost 12; encryption at rest; minimal data held; no payment data; no special category data; MFA on admin access; parameterised SQL throughout; no file upload surface | Low |
| R3 | **A user types a colleague's personal data into a retro note** | Medium | Medium | *Design change made:* the assessment has no free-text field at all; the retro board carries an inline warning; the terms prohibit it; boards are private to one account and never published; the user is controller for third-party data they enter | Low |
| R4 | **Activity log becomes de facto surveillance of individuals** | Medium | Medium | *Design change made:* log limited to account-security events only — no per-page browsing history; IP addresses hashed not stored; {{RETENTION_AUDIT}}-day expiry; anonymised on deletion; fully visible to the data subject; documented LIA with an objection route | Low |
| R5 | Consent for cookies is not freely given because the banner nudges | Low | Medium | *Design change made:* reject and accept are visually identical and equally prominent; one click each; the banner does not block content; no dark patterns; no re-prompting for six months; granular per-purpose control | Low |
| R6 | Data retained longer than needed because nobody runs the cleanup | Medium | Low | Retention implemented as code in `scripts/retention.js`, documented in a published schedule, intended to run as a scheduled job; each run logs what it deleted | Low |
| R7 | Deletion is incomplete — orphaned rows survive | Low | High | Foreign keys with `ON DELETE CASCADE` and `foreign_keys = ON`; deletion runs in a single transaction; a smoke test asserts that no row referencing the user id remains after deletion | Low |
| R8 | Sub-processor changes silently introduce an international transfer | Low | Medium | Published sub-processor list reviewed annually and before any change; transfer mechanism recorded per processor | Low |
| R9 | Users cannot tell what we hold, so cannot exercise rights meaningfully | Low | Medium | Self-service export in both machine-readable and human-readable form; a privacy dashboard showing counts per data category; published ROPA | Very low |

## 8. What would change this assessment

Any of the following would require the DPIA to be redone before shipping:

- Making retro boards shareable between users. Multi-user boards change R3 substantially and introduce a genuine risk of recording opinions about named colleagues.
- Adding third-party analytics, or any script from another domain.
- Adding a free-text field to the assessment.
- Aggregating results across organisations in a way that could identify a specific team.
- Taking payment, or processing any special category data for any reason.
